# Historical Time-Travel RPG --- Game Design Instructions

**Working design document · Version 1**

## 1. Core Story

The protagonist is searching for a missing friend or family member: a
scientist who has travelled through history. The scientist believes
India's past achievements, knowledge, trade networks, and influence have
been overlooked or lost. He investigates different historical periods,
leaving notes, logs, and physical clues behind.

At first, the scientist appears to be researching history. Gradually,
the player discovers that he intends to alter the timeline. When he
returns to his own present, the world has changed and is falling apart.
The player must trace what he changed and understand the consequences.

**Storytelling rule:** The player is not travelling through time to
defeat history. They are travelling through history to follow the
scientist. Major historical events continue independently of ordinary
encounters the player wins.

## 2. Design Pillars

1.  **Exploration first:** Environments, objects, ruins, work areas, and
    small discoveries tell much of the story.
2.  **Short, meaningful writing:** Scientist logs should be brief enough
    to read in a few seconds. Avoid long exposition dumps.
3.  **A living world:** Most people should be seen doing ordinary
    things---working, trading, carrying supplies, studying, resting,
    travelling, or caring for others. Not everyone should stop to speak
    to the player.
4.  **Action-RPG combat:** Real-time top-down combat (Like Hades), not turn-based
    battles. 
5.  **Gradual growth:** The protagonist begins inexperienced and learns
    techniques from teachers while gaining levels and equipment.
6.  **Historical care:** Use credible details and avoid presenting
    debated historical claims as certain. Fictional side encounters
    should not be confused with major historical events.
7.  **Consequences without rewriting every event:** Clearing a small
    camp can help nearby people, but it does not decide the outcome of
    an empire, university, or world war.

## 3. Era and Visual Identity

Keep these four colour identities consistent.

### Era 1 --- Persian Empire

-   **Palette:** Yellow, sand, ochre, warm stone.
-   **Possible period:** Achaemenid Persian Empire, approximately
    550--330 BCE.
-   **Environment:** Desert roads, rocky passes, market settlements,
    fortified checkpoints, courtyards, inscriptions, and trade routes.
-   **Story purpose:** Introduce long-distance trade, political power,
    and connections between regions.
-   **Early gameplay:** Rats and small desert wildlife; then more
    dangerous animals, hazards, and human threats.
-   **Historical care:** Do not imply that every local guard or Persian
    person is hostile.

#### Era 1 opening thread: the king's lost riders (decided 2026-10-27)

-   **The king's request:** a hostile band hides in a cave in the hills
    north-east of the waystation. It raided the camp of the king's
    riders; the camp is left abandoned, and one rider survives, caged in
    the band's jail.
-   **The cave (a type B camp):** entrance → bandit lookouts → the raided
    camp → a fork: the jail / the band's camp → steps up to the chief.
-   **The jail's pens** hold riders who died there before their turn on
    the stake (dark, slumped figures); the caged rider is the jail's
    only survivor.
-   **The rescue is optional.** Open the cage and talk to the rider: he
    joins the hero for revenge. Freeing him always sets off an ambush:
    a wave of bandits, with the way back blocked until they are beaten.
    No rescue means no ambush.
-   **The rider as a companion:** an archer who follows the hero and
    steps out to shoot the nearest enemy in the fight. He never shoots one
    that is asleep, hasn't noticed the hero, or is still only suspicious,
    so he never starts a fight the player meant to avoid. Since 2026-10-28
    he can be hurt, can fall, and can be revived (section 7). Once freed he
    joins the hero's squad for the rest of Era 1 (decided 2026-10-27; see
    section 7, "Companions (squad)").
-   **The chief (camp leader):** a charge along a shown line that leaves
    him dazed if he runs into a wall or rock, and a wide axe sweep. At
    half health he becomes enraged (moves and attacks 1.2-1.5x faster).
    Slingers join only once his fight starts. There is no escape from
    him: once he wakes, his room is sealed until he falls.
-   **The ambush** is two bandits rushing the hero, then two slingers
    who keep their distance; once it is beaten, one or two more of the
    band wait on the way out.
-   **The return:** when the chief falls his room opens, but nothing
    moves the hero on until he cuts down the riders' captain, staked
    alive among his executed men; the captain tells how the band took
    the camp and what the chief did to the survivors. Then the hero
    (with the rider, if freed) is brought back to the throne room,
    walks up to the king, and they speak. Without the rider the king's
    quest has a lesser ending, and the rider stays caged.
-   **The scientist's trail** (decided 2026-10-27): before the raid, the
    rider saw a stranger on the road, heading for the ancient ruins north
    of the town, and says so in the return scene. (Without the rider, the
    captain's account reaches the king instead.) At the ruins the hero
    finds boot prints unlike any sandal, an object the stranger dropped,
    and the first scientist log.
-   **More journal entries** (decided 2026-10-28): a record of the empire's
    history in the great hall's library, a rider's account in the cave of
    how the band's chief broke their camp and killed them, and a second
    scientist log near the ruins.
-   **Scorpions** (decided 2026-10-28): a den near the ancient ruins (type A
    wildlife). Their sting poisons, so it keeps hurting for a few seconds
    after it lands (damage over time).

### Era 2 --- Ancient Coastal City (not finalised)

-   **Palette:** Blue, teal, deep water, faded stone.
-   **Concept:** A fictional ancient Indian Ocean port inspired by
    maritime trade and the mysteries associated with places such as
    Muziris or Dwarka.
-   **Environment:** Busy docks in the flourishing period, warehouses,
    boats, markets, coastal paths, and a later submerged or ruined
    version of the location.
-   **Story purpose:** Show maritime trade and international
    connections; let the player revisit a changed place.
-   **Historical care:** Do not claim that the legendary city of Dwarka
    or the historical port of Muziris is definitively the exact city
    shown underwater. Keep the fictional setting distinct from
    archaeological evidence.

### Era 3 --- Nalanda

-   **Palette:** Green, forest, moss, brick red, muted earth.
-   **Environment:** Monastery courtyards, brick corridors, study areas,
    libraries, ponds, gardens, and secluded forest paths.
-   **Story purpose:** Show Nalanda as a flourishing scholarly and
    monastic centre, then later let the player explore its damaged
    aftermath.
-   **Gameplay:** Explore, find records, assist people, and investigate
    what is missing.
-   **Historical care:** Do not treat debated details about the
    destruction or burning of Nalanda as fully settled. Combat
    encounters must not falsely imply that the player caused or
    prevented its historical fate.

### Era 4 --- Wartime India / WWII

-   **Palette:** Red, black, charcoal, rust, dark steel.
-   **Environment:** Rail yards, depots, barricades, factories, supply
    stores, military facilities, searchlights, and civilian
    neighbourhoods.
-   **Story purpose:** Show wartime pressure, colonial structures,
    disrupted lives, and the human cost of conflict through specific
    places and people.
-   **Side-quest example:** A hungry civilian has no food. The player
    finds food and brings it back; a gesture or short line is enough.
-   **Historical care:** Do not suggest every person experienced the war
    in the same way. The player does not determine the outcome of WWII.

### Optional historical transition --- Portuguese arrival

The arrival of Vasco da Gama at Calicut in 1498 could be a separate
chapter or a shorter story sequence about maritime trade becoming
entangled with armed commercial power. This is not yet a locked chapter.
WWII remains a strong later-era setting.

### Final setting --- Altered present

The scientist returns to a present that no longer matches the one he
left. Familiar locations may be damaged, empty, distorted, or
repurposed. The player follows his final clues and discovers the
consequences of his changes.

## 4. Logs, Story Progression, and NPC Behaviour

### Scientist logs

-   Logs are the main progression mechanism: find a log or clue to
    understand where to go next.
-   A log can reveal a destination, explain why the scientist came to an
    era, or expose a contradiction in his assumptions.
-   Logs should be short, personal, and specific---not textbook
    summaries.
-   Early logs focus on curiosity and research. Later logs reveal
    urgency, obsession, and a willingness to change history.
-   Logs can be incomplete or mistaken. The player should compare them
    with what the environment shows.
-   Use physical clues as well: maps, abandoned equipment, marked
    objects, altered records, and traces of recent activity.

### NPCs and environmental storytelling

-   Most NPCs continue their routines instead of initiating dialogue.
-   Use brief ambient animations: carrying baskets, repairing equipment,
    teaching, studying, cooking, trading, walking, or resting.
-   Only selected NPCs offer clues, teach skills, or give optional
    tasks.
-   Dialogue should be short. Gestures, props, and the environment can
    communicate emotion.
-   Violence is shown by its traces (stains, drag marks, dropped gear, a
    damaged camp, empty instruments of a jail), not by bodies.
-   The one exception is a villain's lair where the cruelty is the point
    (the Era 1 cave chief's room): there, the executed may be shown as
    distant, featureless silhouettes bound to upright stakes (the
    Achaemenid punishment, as at Behistun; never crosses), with no faces
    or wounds. A survivor can tell what happened.
-   The same lair's jail may also show its dead (decided 2026-10-27): dark,
    featureless figures slumped in the small pens, never moving and with
    nothing to interact with, no faces or wounds. Everywhere else the
    blood itself (pools, spatter, drag marks) carries the violence.
-   Side quests should show everyday life and give small human moments,
    not deliver long history lectures.
-   Example: helping a hungry civilian by returning food; the reward may
    be a small item, a useful hint, or simply a changed scene.

### Main progression loop

Explore → notice something unusual → find a clue or help someone →
discover the scientist's next lead → unlock a route or new area → face a
new challenge.

## 5. Three Types of Enemy Camps

These are gameplay categories, not labels for entire historical peoples.
Use them selectively; not every area needs a camp.

### Type A --- Wildlife dens and hazards

-   Examples: rat nests, burrows, aggressive animals protecting a den,
    or dangerous creatures around water.
-   Purpose: introduce basic combat, enemy warning signs, dodging, and
    environmental awareness.
-   Rewards: small supplies, crafting materials if crafting is included,
    or a safe route.
-   Not every animal must be hostile; some should flee or ignore the
    player.

### Type B --- Bandit, smuggler, or raider camps

-   Examples: road bandits in the desert, smugglers near a port, or
    looters exploiting an abandoned supply area.
-   Purpose: introduce groups, patrol routes, ranged enemies, optional
    stealth, and protecting travellers.
-   Rewards: supplies, keys, maps, equipment, or a clue about the
    scientist.
-   Give the group a local motivation; do not make every person in the
    era an enemy.

### Type C --- Guarded outposts or story-linked facilities

-   Examples: a restricted checkpoint, a guarded depot, or a facility
    connected to the scientist's research.
-   Purpose: provide tougher encounters, mixed enemy types,
    environmental hazards, and major progression gates.
-   Rewards: access to a new area, an important clue, or a route around
    an obstacle.
-   These should be used carefully in historical settings. The most
    direct scientist-related facilities can become more prominent later
    in the game.

Possible resolutions include fighting, sneaking past, distracting
guards, finding an alternate route, or completing a task. Combat should
not always be mandatory.

## 6. Enemy Types

Introduce enemies gradually. Early enemies teach one mechanic at a time.

### Early game

-   **Small rats:** quick, low health, simple bite or lunge; some flee.
-   **Larger aggressive animals:** stronger attacks with a clear
    wind-up.
-   **Desert wildlife:** situational hazards or enemies appropriate to
    the chosen area.
-   **Environmental hazards:** falling rocks, unstable floors, currents,
    traps, or dangerous terrain. These can challenge the player without
    requiring a living enemy.

### Mid-game human enemies

-   **Melee attacker:** closes distance and swings; teaches spacing and
    dodging.
-   **Shielded defender:** blocks frontal attacks; encourages flanking
    or a trained guard-breaking move.
-   **Ranged attacker:** fires from a distance; teaches movement and
    using cover.
-   **Scout or patrol:** notices the player and can alert nearby
    enemies.
-   **Heavy opponent:** slow, high-impact attacks with clear warning
    animations.
-   **Camp leader:** combines familiar moves and has more health or a
    unique pattern.

### Later-game threats

-   **Armoured or coordinated groups:** require positioning and
    prioritising targets.
-   **Security devices and traps:** connect to the scientist's research
    where appropriate.
-   **Bosses:** memorable encounters tied to a location or story clue,
    not arbitrary historical figures.

Avoid making scholars, ordinary civilians, or entire historical
communities into enemy classes. Enemies should be individuals or small
groups whose actions create a believable immediate danger.

## 7. Weapons and Equipment

Weapons and tools should add new actions, not only increase damage. The
exact inventory can be refined as the game develops.

-   **Unarmed/basic actions:** the player begins inexperienced; allow a
    simple shove or evasive move, not expert combat.
-   **Rope or lasso:** pull light objects, use designated anchor points,
    interrupt certain attacks, or briefly restrain suitable small
    enemies. Heavy enemies resist the plain throw. The strong lasso
    (held, costs focus) pulls any enemy but a boss, heavy ones too
    (decided 2026-10-29).
-   **Sword:** a reliable close-range weapon introduced after a training
    sequence. In Era 1 (decided 2026-10-28) the first sword comes from
    Veteran Datis's side quest: the hero finds its broken blade and hilt in
    the storeroom, Datis puts it together and gives it, then asks for ten
    of the band's men; coming back to him gives the next reward. Sword
    skills from Datis come later. It is a plain "Sword", not a named
    period blade. Key 1 goes through the weapons carried, and the
    HUD's bottom right shows the weapon in hand. Its special (decided
    2026-10-29) is a flurry of fencing thrusts that leaves foes
    bleeding; the blade flickers with fire while it runs, only so the
    special reads apart from plain swings (it does not burn).
-   **Spear:** longer reach and a different attack rhythm. In Era 1 it is
    bought at Scribe Nidu's stall for 800 coins, costly on purpose
    (decided 2026-10-29).
-   **Bow:** ranged attacks. Arrows are unlimited (decided 2026-10-29);
    the easy damage is held in check by the stamina a draw costs and a
    volley that must be earned by hits and focus. In Era 1 it is bought
    at Nidu's stall for 1200 coins.
-   **Shield:** blocks suitable attacks and may enable a parry after
    training.
-   **Period-appropriate ranged weapons:** later settings can introduce
    historically appropriate firearms where suitable, changing combat
    toward cover, range, and ammunition management.
-   **Armour and protective gear:** improve defence or resist specific
    hazards.
-   **Special scientist-related tool:** a rare device can reveal hidden
    clues or interact with unusual obstacles across eras.

### Equipment rules

-   The weapon's era should affect its appearance and use, but newer
    does not automatically mean better in every situation.
-   Some rare items remain useful across multiple chapters.
-   A lasso or exploration tool can unlock new routes, not just damage
    enemies.
-   Avoid overwhelming the player with too many weapons at once. Maybe era related weapons that should be forfeited before leaving that era.
-   Make pickups, upgrades, and equipment readable in the inventory.

### Inventory and menus (decided 2026-10-27)

-   Menus (inventory, quests, squad, saving) look the same in every era:
    a plain, era-neutral panel, not an era-styled parchment.
-   The inventory groups tools, supplies (food and potions that restore
    HP), valuables, rare finds and story items.
-   The Inventory tab becomes a **Player** tab (decided 2026-10-28): the
    hero's level, XP and bars at the top, then his weapons (each with its
    own level) and his items, in one list.

### Items, money and drops (decided 2026-10-28)

-   **Healing tiers:** Bread (the least), Apple, Small Potion (about 75 HP),
    Large Potion (about 150 HP). Game words are fine here; descriptions keep
    them grounded (herbs in a clay flask, no magic).
-   **Focus potions and the Reviving Potion** (decided 2026-10-28): focus
    potions (the player's "mana") refill focus, in a small and a large size.
    The Reviving Potion is costly and only ever gets a fallen companion back
    up, with little HP (section 7). The descriptions stay grounded like the
    healing potions.
-   **Antidote** (decided 2026-10-28): sold in the shop; it cures poison.
-   **Prices:** Bread 10, Apple 25, and so on up the tiers. The higher the
    tier, the better the value: more HP per coin.
-   **Money:** plain yellow **Coins** for now, not a named period coin. Each
    era may get its own coins later.
-   **Drops:** ordinary enemies drop coins or healing items at random;
    bosses always drop a rare item.
-   **Rare finds** (boss drops) give a small lasting bonus while carried
    and can be sold for coins, so keeping one is a choice.

### Companions (squad) (decided 2026-10-27)

-   Companions belong to their era and never follow the hero into another
    era. The squad is kept per era, not per map.
-   Three orders: **Follow**; **Wait** (hold this spot while the hero is
    on this map; going through a door brings the companion along again);
    **Disband** (the companion goes to a home spot in the era and
    stays in the squad; talk to them there to have them follow again).
-   A companion the story still needs (for example one being escorted to
    safety) cannot be disbanded until that part of the quest is done.
    Farnaka can't be disbanded between his rescue and the return to the
    king.
-   Leaving an era sends every companion of that era to their home spot,
    even one told to Wait.
-   **Companions in a fight** (decided 2026-10-28):
    -   **Follow** means the companion stays with the hero out of a fight
        and moves freely in one, keeping his distance as an archer. When
        the fight is over he comes back to the hero.
    -   **Wait** means he holds his spot in a fight or not, even while he
        is being hurt.
    -   A fight starts only when an enemy has spotted the hero or the hero
        strikes first. A companion never starts one.
    -   An enemy in the fight goes after whoever is nearest, the hero or a
        companion, and the hero wins a close call. Enemies only ever notice
        the hero.
-   **Companions can be hurt** (decided 2026-10-28):
    -   A companion has HP and a focus bar. A full focus bar fires a
        special; Farnaka's is a volley of three arrows.
    -   At 0 HP he falls and stays down, even through doors, until the hero
        uses a **Reviving Potion** on him or rests.
    -   The potion is costly and brings him back with little HP.
    -   The hero is never revived: when he falls, he falls.
    -   The hero can use the potion beside the companion or from the menu.
-   Food and potions (healing or focus) can be given to a companion from
    the menu, in the Player tab or the Squad tab (decided 2026-10-28).
-   Equipment and more members (the riders' captain, hired guards) come
    later.
-   Later in the story, when a great threat arises, heroes from every era
    are called to join together.

## 8. Combat System

### Chosen direction

A **top-down real-time action RPG** where exploration and combat happen on the same map.

1. **Exploration mode:** The player explores the world while enemies roam, patrol, guard territory, or remain near their home locations.
2. **Combat:** When an enemy detects or engages the player, real-time combat begins directly on the exploration map. There is no transition into a separate combat arena.

Both exploration and combat use the same top-down perspective and movement controls. The player can escape ordinary encounters by leaving enemy territory and breaking pursuit.

Boss encounters take place in specially designed areas of the world, but remain part of the same map. Specific boss encounters may restrict escape when appropriate.

### Encounter flow

1. The player sees enemies in the world and can observe, sneak past, or avoid them.
2. Enemy detection or contact triggers combat on the current map.
3. Enemies pursue the player according to their patrol routes, home territory, and pursuit limits.
4. Nearby enemies may join if they detect the player or become alerted.
5. The player can defeat enemies, disengage, or escape ordinary encounters.
6. Victory grants experience and possibly loot. Escaping does not grant victory rewards.
7. Health, level, equipment, and skills persist throughout exploration and combat.

### Basic combat controls

-   Move with the same directional controls in both modes. Attacks aim
    eight ways, wherever the move keys last pointed.
-   Light and heavy attacks (Mouse 1 / Mouse 2), played as each weapon's
    named strings (for example the fists' punch, punch, kick); a finished
    string shows its name (decided 2026-10-29, tried in the combat arena).
-   Block (Space held; part of a frontal hit gets through, at a stamina
    cost) and parry (Space pressed just before a hit). A few heavy boss
    moves and thrown stones go through the block.
-   A special ability on Q, each weapon its own (the fists keep the
    lasso), paid for with focus and followed by a recharge; Q after a
    string's second swing ends it as a named finisher.
-   Dodge or evade.
-   Interact/use item.
-   For now every string, special and the parry are available from the
    start (decided 2026-10-29); later they are taught by teachers, so the
    control set grows as the player learns.

### Enemy behaviour

- Enemies have patrol routes, home locations, or defined territories during exploration.
- Hostile enemies react to proximity or sight where appropriate.
- Cover: tall things (walls, buildings, cliffs, pillars, big rocks) hide the player from an enemy's sight. Low things the player still
  cannot walk through (nests, dead trees, cacti, fallen column drums, small scattered rocks and plants) give no cover.
- Enemies chase the player only within their assigned pursuit limits.
- If the player escapes far enough, enemies disengage and return to their own patrol routes or home locations.
- An enemy that walks back to its home area heals back to full when it gives up the chase. This includes bosses (decided 2026-10-27).
  A fall now loads a save (section 11), so after a fall in a boss fight the boss is back at full health, as the save left him.
- Ordinary enemies come back when the hero walks into a map again or rests (decided 2026-10-28). Bosses never come back, and
  scripted fights (an ambush, a boss's helpers) are not repeated. A cleared hideout refills like any other place (in Era 1 the cave's
  bandits return even after the chief has fallen). The story never needs these extra fights.
- Nearby members of the same group may join combat when they detect the player or receive an alert.
  Decided 2026-10-28: once one enemy is alerted (it spotted the hero, or he struck it), every enemy of its kind of group nearby stops
  patrolling and joins the fight.
- Different enemy types should teach different responses.
- Some enemies flee, guard an area, or can be avoided; not everything should attack on sight.

### Independent Factions and Territories

- Each enemy group has its own home location, patrol routes, and pursuit boundary.
- Guard posts and bandit camps operate independently; they do not share one territory just because they are nearby.
- If a bandit group notices a guard post or its guards, its response depends on that group's behaviour: it may avoid the post or retreat toward its own camp instead of approaching the guards.
- Guards do not automatically control bandit territory, and bandits do not automatically enter or take over a guard post.
- Different factions may ignore, oppose, or cooperate with one another when the encounter design calls for it.
- One faction may react to a fight or alarm involving another faction, but this must be deliberately configured rather than happening everywhere automatically.
- If the player escapes, each group returns to its own patrol route or home location, not another faction's post.

### Combat Areas and Enemy Territories

- Combat happens directly on the exploration map; do not load a separate combat arena.
- Enemies have defined home territories or pursuit limits that control how far they can chase the player.
- These limits restrict enemy AI, not the player's movement.
- Wildlife stays near nests or habitats; patrols follow routes; guards defend posts; and camps can have larger territories.
- Nearby members of the same group can join a fight when alerted, provided they are within a reasonable distance or territory.
- Use the environment—rocks, walls, barricades, buildings, and machinery—to create natural combat spaces.
- Boss encounters may use specially designed sections of the world, such as a restricted road or battlefield, without switching maps.
- Keep enemy pursuit and disengagement predictable so the player understands when escape is possible.

### Build complexity gradually

Prototype only:

1. One small desert exploration map.
2. One roaming rat enemy with a defined home territory.
3. Enemy detection and pursuit limits.
4. Combat on the exploration map.
5. Basic movement, attack, health, and damage.
6. One readable enemy lunge and a dodge.
7. Enemy disengagement when the player escapes.
8. Experience and a basic reward.

Then add more enemies, group alerts, independent faction behaviour, guard posts, a sword, teachers, parrying, ranged enemies, camps, and bosses in later steps.

### Combat balance (added 2026-10-27)

How enemy health, weapon damage and XP fit together, and what changing
them does to a fight. The exact numbers live in the code (the README's
"Where to tune" lists them), and the dev tuning panel (T) changes them
while playing.

-   **The hero's bars** (decided 2026-10-28): **HP 100**, **stamina 100**
    and **focus 50** at the start. Every swing, dodge and throw spends
    stamina, and what a move costs depends on the weapon (a sword's swing
    will cost more than a punch). It refills slowly once the hero stops
    spending (about 8 s from empty to full; decided 2026-10-28, because the
    first refill was too quick). Focus is spent only on special moves (the strong lasso, each
    weapon's special and finishers, the bow's volley; later a firearm's shot) and fills by landing
    hits, more for a full combo, and by a parry. It never refills by itself (decided
    2026-10-28). Levels raise all three (section 9).
-   **The hero** has 1 s of safety after a hit. Most enemy hits cost 20 HP;
    a boss's big move (the giant rat's lunge and slam, the chief's charge)
    costs 40. A beginner can afford about four mistakes in a fight.
-   **Damage per hit:** punch 10, punch 10, kick 20. A full combo deals 40
    in about 0.8 s, with a short pause after the kick, and costs 40 of the
    hero's 100 stamina. The rider's arrow deals 10 every 0.5 s (about 20
    damage a second).
-   **Boss fights** show the boss's name and health in a wide bar at the
    bottom of the screen (decided 2026-10-28).
-   **Enemies today** (unarmed only; "swinging" is non-stop swinging, so a
    real fight takes several times longer, because you hit in the openings
    after a lunge, charge or sweep):

| Enemy | HP | Punches | Combos | Swinging | Rider alone | XP |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Slinger | 20 | 2 | 0.5 | 0.4 s | 1 s | 15 |
| Rat | 30 | 3 | 0.75 | 0.8 s | 1.5 s | 10 |
| Bandit | 60 | 6 | 1.5 | 1.5 s | 3 s | 25 |
| Giant rat | 120 | 12 | 3 | 2.8 s | 6 s | 100 |
| Chief | 200 | 20 | 5 | 4.9 s | 10 s | 150 |

-   **Rules of thumb:** an ordinary enemy dies within about two combos
    (one or two openings). A boss takes four to six combos, so the player
    sees each of its moves several times. Enemies that come in groups
    (slingers, summoned rats) die in one or two hits.
-   **What changing a number does:**
    -   **Enemy HP:** every 40 HP (one combo) more means one more opening to
        wait for, so one more chance to be hit. Below one combo, an enemy is
        a single exchange.
    -   **Hero damage:** scales every fight at once. A stronger kick
        rewards finishing the combo; a stronger punch rewards hit-and-run.
    -   **The rider's arrows:** with him along, most fights take about half
        as long. The bosses' HP was set without him in mind.
    -   **XP:** changes how fast the hero levels up (section 9).
    -   **Stamina costs:** dearer moves make the hero pause between
        combos; cheap ones let him swing and dodge almost freely.
    -   **Enemy damage** (not on the panel): 20 more HP per hit cuts the
        mistakes a beginner can afford from about four to two.

## 9. Character Progression and Teachers

The protagonist starts as an ordinary person, not a trained fighter.

### Progression layers

-   **Character level:** earned through combat, quests, discoveries, and
    exploration challenges.
-   **Stats:** modest improvements to health, stamina, or damage.
-   **Skills:** unlocked or improved through skill points and training.
-   **Equipment:** found, earned through quests, or recovered from
    suitable locations.
-   **Knowledge/tools:** unlock new interactions and exploration routes.

### Levels and weapon XP (decided 2026-10-28)

-   **Two kinds of XP.** The hero's level comes from kills, main-story
    steps, side quests and journal logs; getting past enemies without a
    fight will be rewarded later. Each level raises max HP, stamina and
    focus and fills them. Separately, **each weapon has its own XP**,
    starting at 0 and filled by using it; punches and kicks are the
    **Unarmed** weapon. A weapon's levels make that weapon stronger (more
    damage, a quicker or longer lasso). They never unlock a new move by
    themselves: moves still come from teachers (section 13).
-   **Level cap.** The hero's level stops at a cap (10 to begin with), and
    only specific teachers can raise it. Weapon levels have caps that work
    the same way.
-   **Enemy levels.** Every enemy has a level. A foe below the hero's
    level gives less XP, so fighting weak enemies again (they respawn,
    section 8) cannot level the hero quickly. Story, side-quest and log
    XP is never reduced.

### Teachers

-   A teacher introduces a weapon or technique through a short task or
    training challenge.
-   Finding a weapon does not automatically mean the protagonist knows
    how to use it well.
-   Example: find a sword, meet a suitable teacher, complete a task,
    then unlock basic sword attacks. Later training unlocks a counter or
    stronger strike.
-   Teachers can be hunters, veterans, craftspeople, guards who are
    willing to help, or other credible characters for the setting.
-   Not every era needs a teacher for every weapon.

### Rewards and balance

-   Reward clever avoidance and completing objectives, not only kills.
-   Do not require grinding to progress through the story.
-   Use equipment to change play style as well as stats.
-   Keep early enemies dangerous enough to teach, but forgiving enough
    for a beginner.

## 10. Quests, Rewards, and Historical Consequences

-   Main quests follow the scientist's trail and unlock the next
    destination.
-   Side quests show everyday life, help NPCs, or reveal optional
    history and clues.
-   Rewards can include experience, equipment, training, a shortcut, a
    small supply, or a new log.
-   Avoid making every quest a combat task.
-   A local problem can be resolved without changing a major historical
    outcome.
-   Revisit locations when useful to show change over time: a thriving
    port and its later ruins, or Nalanda flourishing and its later
    damaged state.
-   Treat historical events with nuance. The protagonist's victories in
    small encounters should not rewrite major events.

## 11. Audio, Visual Feedback, and Accessibility

-   Give attacks clear wind-up animations, sound cues, and impact
    feedback.
-   Make the player's health and enemy danger readable without
    cluttering the screen.
-   Distinguish interactable objects and scientist clues from
    decoration.
-   Use the era palette consistently so each chapter is recognisable at
    a glance.
-   Allow players to understand an enemy's attack from animation and
    positioning, not colour alone.
-   Keep log text short and provide readable UI.
-   Combat should feel responsive; avoid adding elaborate effects before
    the core movement and hit detection work.

### HUD and saving (decided 2026-10-27)

-   The current objective sits at the top left; the hero's bars (HP with
    its number, stamina, focus), then XP, are stacked under it. The foes
    slain are not on the HUD (decided 2026-10-28): the journal lists each
    kind of enemy met, with a short description and the number slain.
-   Entering a named place shows its name at the top right for a few
    seconds. Only well-known places are named (a town, the ruins, the
    king's courtyard, the great hall, a whole cave), not rooms inside one.
-   Saving: one autosave and four save slots. The autosave is written at
    rest spots (which also fill the hero's bars), on going through a
    door, and when the main objective moves on. The four slots are saved
    and loaded from the menu.
-   Rest spots are few: in Era 1 the town well and the fire before the
    cave's boss room. A rest spot can only be used while no enemy is
    after the hero.
-   A save keeps the HP the hero had; loading (also after dying) starts
    with it.
-   **The death screen** (decided 2026-10-28): when the hero falls, the
    screen says so and where, and the player picks the save to go on
    from (the autosave or a filled slot), or a new game. There is no
    getting up where he fell: a fall costs whatever happened since that
    save.
-   The journal (logs and clues) has its own menu tab, apart from the
    quests.
-   Menu keys (decided 2026-10-28): I opens the inventory (the Player
    tab), M the map, Tab the squad, Esc the system tab. The Map tab is
    blank until maps can be had.

## 13. Non-Negotiable Design Rules

-   Keep the visual identities: **yellow/sand → blue/underwater → green/forest → red/black/industry**.
-   Use visible enemies and real-time top-down combat; no turn-based
    battle screens.
-   Keep the same movement controls in exploration and combat.
-   Scientist logs and environmental clues advance the main story.
-   Most NPCs continue their work; do not make everyone talk.
-   Start with simple enemies, such as rats and wildlife, and introduce
    complexity gradually.
-   Teachers unlock skills; weapons do not automatically grant mastery.
-   Major historical events should not depend on defeating ordinary
    enemies.
-   Build a small playable prototype before expanding every era.
-   Enemies have defined patrol routes, home territories, and pursuit limits. Different factions have independent territories and reactions.
-   Allow escape from ordinary encounters; boss encounters may have special restrictions, and so may scripted story encounters
    (e.g. the ambush after the rescue in the Era 1 cave, and the cave chief's sealed room).
